CRISPR-Cas9 is an adaptive immune mechanism, originally found in bacteria and archaea, that has been repurposed as a programmable genome-editing tool. The system uses a single guide RNA (sgRNA) to direct the Cas9 endonuclease to a specific DNA sequence, where Cas9 introduces a double-strand break. The break is then repaired by the cell, either through error-prone non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), which can disrupt a gene, or through homology-directed repair (HDR), which can introduce precise edits when a donor template is supplied. Target specificity is determined by a 20-nucleotide spacer in the sgRNA and by the requirement for a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) immediately downstream of the target. Off-target cleavage remains a central concern for therapeutic applications; high-fidelity Cas9 variants and base or prime editing have been developed to improve precision. CRISPR-Cas9 has accelerated functional genomics, disease modeling, and the development of gene therapies.
